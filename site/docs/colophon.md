# The colophon and the site's standing

**Can. 216 CIC** reserves the name _Catholic_ to undertakings holding the
consent of competent ecclesiastical authority. This site is named `Glossa
Catholica`, holds no such consent, and said nothing anywhere about its standing
until 2026-09-02. `colophon.whatThisIsStanding` says it now: a private
undertaking of the lay faithful, no ecclesiastical approbation, no authority of
its own.

**The word is not the exposure; the absence of a disclaimer was.** The
commentary tradition reads _vindicare_ narrowly — what is forbidden is claiming
the name so as to imply the undertaking acts in the Church's name — and on the
facts here that reading was the unfavourable one. A site that reproduces the
Catechism and the magisterial documents verbatim and adds no visible byline
presents as an organ of the Holy See's own texts. That confusion is the whole
_ratio legis_, and it existed independently of the title. (Compare c. 808,
which concedes that a thing may _be_ Catholic in fact and still not be entitled
to the _title_: being manifestly orthodox is not a defence.)

**It sits in "What this is", not in the copyright section.** The copyright
section is addressed to a rights holder; standing is addressed to the reader.
It is not its own `<h2>` because a heading would need a name in thirty-four
dictionaries for a term of art none of them has a settled word for.

**What it does NOT say is what is ours, and that omission is deliberate.** A
paragraph claiming the arrangement and the editorial descriptions was drafted
and removed. There is real prose here that is not the publishers' — but a
reader meets that prose _beside a document_, so a paragraph on a page most
readers never open cannot mark the individual item, which is the only place the
question arises. Provenance belongs in the reading interface, per item
(`PLAN.md` gap 16), and until it exists the colophon claims nothing rather than
claiming it in the weakest available place.

**The page is translated, which reversed a standing rule.** The long colophon
prose had been withheld from every dictionary, on the argument that a machine
translation of the page explaining how carefully this site handles other
people's words would be the one page whose form contradicts its content. **That
argument proves too much**: the page's job is to be _weighed_ by the reader it
is addressed to, and a reader who cannot read it cannot weigh it. An English
wall is not more honest than a translation, only more silent.

**What the old rule was protecting is kept, and moved.** Each dictionary states
its own confidence tier, and two colophon strings are singled out there because
they are OPERATIVE rather than descriptive — the standing statement, and
`copyrightBody3`, which tells a rights holder how to object. Everywhere else a
bad translation reads oddly; in those two it would misstate what this site
claims about itself, or lose someone the only sentence written for them.
Deleting a doubtful line remains the right fix, since `t()` falls back per key.

**The statement reached the footer of every page**, which is the same argument
one step on rather than a second decision: can. 216 is provoked by the NAME,
and the name is in the wordmark at every address, so a disclaimer only a reader
who navigates to the colophon meets does not reach as far as the thing it
disclaims. `footer.notEndorsed` says **"the Holy See"** rather than "the
Vatican", which names the state and not the authority. **It is as short as it
is because of where it sits**: the line above it links to the full statement,
so it need not carry its own context, and the three lines are deliberately the
same size and colour — set the motto larger and the stack reads as a heading
with two captions rather than as an imprint.

**Neither of the two marks beside it is encumbered, and that was checked rather
than assumed.** _Ad maiorem Dei gloriam_ long ago passed into general Catholic
use; the Jerusalem Cross is a public Christian symbol older than either body
that uses it today. **Can. 216 reserves a NAME. No canon reserves a symbol or a
Latin phrase.** The one line not to cross is a specific body's ARMS — no crown,
no motto ring, no red-on-white in the Order's arrangement — because a mark
drifting there would contradict the sentence printed next to it. The
component's docblock carries that rule, so making it look "more official" has
to be a decision someone takes rather than a tidy-up.

**The mark itself is not ours, and that is the right answer rather than a
shortcut.** Three drawings were made here first, each an attempt to settle
proportions heraldry settled centuries ago. What ships is Wikimedia Commons'
`Cross-Jerusalem-Potent-Heraldry.svg`, public domain, checked through the
Commons API before copying — and credited in the docblock regardless, because a
site whose whole position is about other people's rights does not take a
licence exemption as permission to go quiet about where something came from.
Its `<use xlink:href>` indirection was expanded into explicit rotations (two
copies of a component carrying `id`s are duplicate ids in one document) and the
result pixel-diffs to **zero** against the original render: **a refactor of
someone else's geometry is a redraw unless something checks it.**

**Two canons bite harder than 216 and are not addressed by any of this**,
recorded here so nobody reads the colophon as having settled them. **C. 826
§3**: collections of prayers for public or private use are not to be published
except with the permission of the local ordinary — `/preces` is exactly that.
**C. 825 §1**: Scripture may not be published without the approval of the
Apostolic See or the conference of bishops; Challoner and the Clementine carry
historical approbations, and `bible.cpdv.en` carries none at all.
